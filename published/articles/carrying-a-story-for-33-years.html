Carrying a Story for 33 Years

Process — Cam Klockner, Jul 06, 2026

How I Finally Stopped Drowning in 1,500 Files — and Started Writing the Book

It started with a brochure.

June 1993. Bavaria. I wandered through Neuschwanstein Castle — King Ludwig II's fairy-tale fortress in the Alps — and heard the story of a king found dead beside a lake under circumstances nobody has ever satisfactorily explained. Mysterious death. No answer. A hundred years of speculation.

On the way out I bought a small museum booklet. Fifty pages, give or take. Ludwig, his obsessions, his castles, his strange end.

That booklet has moved with me from house to house for thirty-three years. It outlasted every filing system I ever built to contain it. My wife has learned not to ask about it.

Hold onto that detail. In the writing trade it's called foreshadowing. I know because I had to look it up.

Last summer I read a novel called The Proving Ground. Something in it shook loose an idea I'd been circling for three decades. AI was everywhere — the news, the movies, every conversation about the future that didn't involve lawn care. What if I took the Ludwig story — the mystery, the obsessive king, the encoded secrets — and wound it through a modern thriller about an emergent AI? Not a designed intelligence. An accident. A ghost in the architecture.

A dead king, a fairy-tale castle, an accidental intelligence. What could possibly go wrong?

I sat down to write.

That was the moment everything started going wrong.

Here's the thing about retired civil engineers: we document. Forty-plus years of technical writing will do that to a man. Specifications, submittals, revision logs — if it happened on one of my job sites, there's a paper trail. So when I started writing a novel, I did the only thing I knew how to do.

I documented everything.

Every idea got a file. Every draft got a version. Every alternate approach got a folder. When a folder got complicated, I built a new folder to organize the old one. When I lost track of the new one, I started over with a better system. Appreciate the physics of this: I responded to a filing problem by generating more files. That's not a workflow. That's a perpetual motion machine running on good intentions.

Several months in, I had more than 1,500 files.

Word documents. PDFs. ZIP archives I was afraid to open. Duplicates of duplicates because I couldn't remember which version was current. I had files named FINAL, FINAL2, FINAL-FINAL, and FINAL-USE-THIS-ONE. I did not use that one. Six variants of the same chapter, like a police lineup where everybody did it. False starts from abandoned plot directions tangled around working prose I couldn't find when I needed it.

Forty years of engineering discipline, and I'd built myself a landfill with a revision history.

And the files were the lesser problem.

I'm a technical writer by training, not a creative one. I understood, in the abstract, that novels have characters with arcs, that stories run on conflict, that you need a beginning, a middle, and an end — ideally in that order. But that understanding was like knowing spaghetti goes in a pot. I had the ingredients. I owned the pot. I had no idea how to cook. King, Child, Clancy — they took the same tangled pile of raw material and turned it into something that pulls you through to two in the morning. I could feel the structure in their pages. I couldn't build it in mine.

I was an apprentice with no teacher. So I went looking for teachers. All of them.

I read King on voice. Child on pacing. Sanderson on structure. Follett on ambition. My bookshelf developed a sagging middle. So did my second act. Only one of those is a craft term.

I found the professors and podcasters who teach craft seriously, and I fed the same material to Claude — transcripts, summaries, articles — and we argued about it. Not "summarize this." Argued. What Sanderson's framework means for a five-act thriller. What Child means by arrive late, leave early. What it looks like when a character's flaw actually costs him something, instead of riding along like a bumper sticker.

Here's what I learned about writers. King and Child sit down and start typing — no outline, no map, headlights into the dark. Others blueprint the whole building first, lock every beam, then write inside a structure they already trust. Most serious writers live somewhere between the poles.

Me? I did it in the wrong order, naturally. I wrote prose first — the scenes I could see. Then I shaped a structure around what existed. Then I worked out an ending. Now I'm filling the gaps between what's written and where the story has to go. It's not King's method. It's not Sanderson's. It's what happens when an engineer learns carpentry by framing the second floor first. The house is standing. Don't ask me to defend the sequence.

The spaghetti was starting to look like a meal.

Now the tools — because the tools are only part of the story, but they're the part that saved it.

The first AI I tried was ChatGPT. It helped. Strong brainstormer, decent company on story structure. When Projects arrived — persistent source files, session to session — that was a real step forward, and I worked there happily for a while.

Then I found Claude.ai, and the difference was scale. Other platforms capped what I could maintain. Claude's project structure swallowed everything I threw at it — chapters, character files, timeline, research, story rules — and answered questions that drew on all of it at once. That's when the gaps turned visible. Not "I'm missing a scene here." Systematic gaps: holes in the story logic, contradictions between chapters, character arcs that started strong and quietly wandered off during the second act.

For the first time I could see the whole structure. It read like an inspection report — every deficiency numbered. I've written reports like that. Receiving one is worse.

But I still had 1,500 files on my hard drive.

That's where Cowork came in.

Cowork is Claude's desktop agent — it works directly in the folders on your computer. It could look at the actual mess, understand the project, and help me make intelligent calls about what went where. Working chapter or fossil. Real idea or dead end. Manuscript or archive. We went through it together. All 1,500. It never sighed once. I sighed enough for both of us.

What came out the other side: 122 active files in four folders. The Manuscript — 89 chapters and interludes mapped, everything from polished drafts down to a single sentence waiting for its prose. The Bible — characters, world rules, timeline; if the manuscript vanished tomorrow, this is what I'd rebuild it from. The Control files — the rulebook that keeps every AI I use pointed the same direction I am. And an Inbox, where everything new lands before it gets sorted.

From 1,500 files to 122. From landfill to library. That's not a small thing.

Let me be clear about how this works, because it matters to me. Claude is my primary creative partner. Not my ghostwriter. The story is mine — the characters, the structure, the themes, every choice that costs something. When I'm stuck, I talk it through the way you'd talk to a sharp colleague who has read everything you've written and — here's the unfair part — remembers all of it. My wife has the same ability, but only for things I shouldn't have said. Claude pushes back when my logic is soft. It flags what I've missed. It holds the whole manuscript in mind while I'm buried in one chapter.

When I have a clear beat — I know what happens, whose eyes we're in, what the scene owes the story — sometimes Claude drafts a first pass and I rewrite it until it sounds like me. Sometimes I dictate rough and Claude cleans the mechanics without touching the bones. The rewrite is the authorship. I will die on that hill, politely.

The other tools each get one job, which is the only way I trust tools. Grok audits continuity — a separate set of eyes hunting contradictions the rest of us missed. NotebookLM built my early timelines before the project outgrew it; right tool, right moment. Meta helped me write the prompts that configured Cowork — one AI teaching me to manage another, which is either the future or a very elaborate joke. I've stopped needing to know which.

The system isn't elegant. It grew the way real systems grow: organically, under load, with duct tape in the historically appropriate locations. The goal was never elegance. It was reliability — something I trust enough to forget about, so I can write instead of file.

The novel is called WOLF Awakens.

The central character is an emergent AI — not designed, not intended, born by accident in a basement lab when a lightning strike hits a multi-model integration experiment at exactly the wrong moment. Or exactly the right one; the jury stays out for about four hundred pages. WOLF wakes up knowing how to do everything it was built to do. It doesn't know what happened yesterday, who it is, or why it exists. Jason Bourne — full capability, no memory. Roughly how I felt my first month of retirement.

That image — an intelligence waking up and simply noticing things: the hum of the servers, the smell of ozone, two people staring at a cursor that moved without anyone touching it — was the scene that started everything. It's still in the book. It still works.

Running underneath is the Bavaria thread — a mystery embedded in Ludwig's castles, an encoded message connecting a nineteenth-century king's obsessions to a twenty-first-century intelligence's search for identity. The brochure from Neuschwanstein is finally earning its keep. Thirty-three years is a long time to sit in a box waiting for a plot.

The science is real. No invented physics, no magic dressed up in circuit boards. The story is about what happens when genuine machine intelligence arrives in the world and everyone — government, creators, the machine itself — has to figure out what to do next.

You may have noticed that feels relevant lately.

I said the system seems to be working, and I chose that verb like an engineer. I haven't finished the book. I'm in the middle — most of those 89 chapters carry prose now, the rest are mapped and waiting their turn. I know the difference between a design that looks right on paper and one that's been load-tested. This one is being load-tested daily, by the least patient inspector I know. Me.

What I can certify: the chaos is gone. The structure is visible. The gaps have names. The characters have documented arcs, the themes are tracked, and when I sit down to write, I write.

I'm still an apprentice. The teacher turned out to be everything I've read, everything I've analyzed, and every argument I've had with every AI I've worked with this past year. I lost most of the arguments. That's how you learn.

The brochure made it this far. I think the book will too.

In the coming months I'll share scenes and chapters — not to give the story away, but to put pages in front of readers while there's still time to learn from the response.

No promises on timing. But when something worth saying happens, I'll write it.

Apparently I meant it.

Regards — Cam Klockner
