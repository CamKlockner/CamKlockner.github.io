Claude, Book Writing, and File Organization

Sequel to "Carrying a Story for 33 Years"

Cam Klockner · July 6, 2026 · revised and published November 16, 2026

The descriptions of Claude's tools and my workflow are outdated in this article. Look for a sequel coming soon.

It's a Tuesday, and I'm driving, and an idea hits me — uninvited, half-formed, gone if I don't catch it. I pull over and dictate it into my phone. Some days that's one line. Some days it's an entire chapter, or a piece of the timeline. An ending had been taunting me for days, and then, at mile marker 105 on the Garden State Parkway, it arrived whole. I pulled over and dictated a rough of the entire chapter — I didn't want to lose it before it evaporated.

That's a spark. This article is about what happens to it next.

Last go 'round I took you through changing a workflow of 1,500 scattered files to a manageable structure: 122 files in four folders. It's 158 now, and it's reshaped constantly as I add, edit, or modify content — it's a book, my first novel (fingers crossed). Every new spark, every scene, every idea changes the count — a chapter added, two chapters consolidated into one, a character detail changed. The count is a moving target. What doesn't move is the process underneath it.

Here are two ways the book grows — from a single spark to a filed chapter.

The Spark

The Parkway ending was a big one. More often it's smaller. Today it's a single line: WOLF — the emergent AI at the center of the book — says something to Reese, its creator, that it shouldn't be able to say yet, not given what WOLF doesn't remember about itself. A small spark, same as the big one, caught the same way.

That's the raw material. Ugly, unpunctuated, missing half its verbs.

An ending had been taunting me for days, and then, at mile marker 105 on the Garden State Parkway, it arrived whole.

Back at the desk, I hand it to Claude.AI and we go back and forth — not "write this for me," but "here's what I heard in my head, help me get it organized enough to say it." Fifteen minutes, or a half hour, a dozen small revisions, and the spark is a scene now. Something book-worthy, maybe.

That's a fix on the old system. I used to save sparks into a haphazard file structure, and as they piled up, I lost track of what was where, when, and with whom. I'd often write the same idea again, differently, and end up with multiple versions competing for the same real estate in the story. Fifteen hundred files, most of them half-remembered sparks with nowhere to go.

Now, I drop it in the Project Inbox.

The Session

The book gets written because I sit down and decide to. The sparks get woven in along the way.

I open a Claude.AI prompt box and say I want to work on C032, a chapter. What comes back isn't a blank page — it's a briefing. Whatever prose already exists for that chapter. Every open gap, pulled straight from the Control folder's gap logs. Every blocker, tagged and specific, not vague, pulled from the same place. Sometimes the note is about the writing itself: I started the scene too soon, or let it run too long past the moment it should have ended. Sometimes it's structural: this chapter owes the story a piece of information it hasn't delivered yet.

Some chapters start with nothing but the idea — no prose on the page yet, just the spark and the briefing built around it. Others already have a full draft waiting, and the work is closing what's still open.

Then I write. Dictate, type, rearrange — whatever the task calls for. Five or six rounds, whatever it takes. Some sessions finish the chapter. Some don't — half of it gets written, saved, and picked back up another day. The briefing picks up exactly where it left off, gaps and all.

After each pass, the response isn't a pat on the back — I built that in on purpose, in the Project Instructions. I ask what I'm doing right, what I'm doing wrong, and how I can improve it. It responds with specifics: this part landed, this part still doesn't close the gap. I build the fix from my own next spark, or a combination of a few Claude.AI suggests. That's the iteration, edging closer to something book-worthy. Sometimes two or three rounds will do it. I recall one afternoon that took a dozen, and still needed sleeping on before I picked it up again the next day. Until I own it.

When it's right, into the Inbox it goes for step two.

The Inbox doesn't care where a spark was born. It only cares that it shows up.

This all happens in Claude.AI Projects — memory from prompt box to prompt box, plus a cache of Source Files, so Claude.AI has everything already written on hand. Uploaded manually, though. Still the biggest drawback in the whole process.

The organizing, the migration, the insertions and integration — all the hard stuff — is done by Claude Cowork, where I also have a project for this novel in progress, WOLF Awakens. Cowork can access the assigned folders and files on my computer. It can edit them, rename them, move them. It runs on the same models Claude.AI does. Same abilities, full stop. Why don't I just do everything in Cowork? Because Cowork uses significantly more tokens for a creative session than Claude.AI does. I write where it's cheap, and save Cowork for the heavy lifting — the organizing, the integration — used as sparingly as possible. That's only part of what Cowork was designed for.

Some sparks don't start in either place. Every so often an idea gets worked out on another AI platform entirely — wherever I happen to be when it surfaces. It comes back as a file and drops into the same Inbox as everything else. The Inbox doesn't care where a spark was born. It only cares that it shows up.

Inbox Files Don't Take Care of Themselves

As new work lands in the Inbox — sparks, finished chapters, or anything in between — Cowork's real work starts. Often, just because I can and because I'm curious, I'll run a spark by a different chat interface first, then copy the result of that exchange into the same Inbox, through File Explorer.

Cowork takes what's accumulated in the Inbox, breaks the files apart, and inserts each piece where it belongs — manuscript, Bible, or Control, wherever it's tagged for, or a new file if none exists yet. Then it updates the Mirror (more in a minute).

Now the plumbing. Six folders. That's the whole machine.

Inbox you already know. Three folders hold the actual book: Manuscript, Bible, Control. Two more keep it honest: Mirror, Archive.

MANUSCRIPT

the chapters. 89 of them right now, 73,000 words, likely to land at 65-70 chapters and 90-100,000 words by the end. Every file here is a spark that made it.

BIBLE

the whole story in miniature. Characters, tech, research, timeline. If the manuscript vanished tomorrow, this is what I'd rebuild it from.

CONTROL

the rulebook. Naming, voice, technique, gap logs, continuity logs. What keeps Claude.AI, Cowork, and any other LLM pointed the same direction I am.

Integration touches one of these, sometimes all three, and files get modified, renamed and replaced.

Nothing gets deleted — except an exact duplicate. Everything else, old prose, retired instructions, abandoned sparks, goes to ARCHIVE, reviewed and then shelved. Not gone.

Last: MIRROR. Not a backup — a flat folder, no subfolders, an exact copy of Manuscript, Bible, and Control. Its whole job is convenience: one folder I can dump into Claude.AI's Source Files in a single upload, no digging through nested paths: old Source Files out, new ones in from the Mirror.

None of this makes me write faster. What it does is keep me from getting distracted by the constant organizational and administrative grind of building a three- to four-hundred-page novel. It closes the gap between the writer I am when a spark hits at full speed and the writer I am three months later, trying to remember whether I already used that idea somewhere else.

None of this held still long enough to finish this article, either. Parts of this piece changed underneath it twice before publishing. The methodology isn't a monument — it's a working document, same as the book. When the tools get better, it gets revised, or it falls behind the thing it was built to support.

Forty years of engineering told me I needed a system, so I built one. It's been a work in progress for over six months, and I finally have something workable. It holds under load and tells me the truth when something doesn't fit. It doesn't write WOLF Awakens for me. It just makes sure every spark gets its chance to become fire.
